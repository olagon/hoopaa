# Word audit, September 2026

Every one of the 1,000 words was checked against Pukui-Elbert (1986) and Māmaka Kaiao, pulled from Wehewehe Wikiwiki (wehe.hilo.hawaii.edu), by eight reviewers working in parallel. An independent second reviewer then re-checked every spelling, meaning and pronunciation change plus a random sample of 60 untouched words. When a spelling or meaning changed, the story, sound hook, say-it action and picture were rewritten so the memory trick teaches the corrected word.

Changes from the first pass: 136 across 136 words. Second pass fixes: 5. Stress mark fixes: 27.

## Words replaced (not a real word with that meaning) (2)

- **v400 hoʻohou** → **hoʻāla**. hoʻohou is not in Pukui-Elbert, Māmaka Kaiao, Andrews, Parker or Judd (WebFetch searches for hoʻohou and hoohou return No entry found). Pukui-Elbert's words for renew are hana hou, hoʻomau hou, hoʻomaka hou and hoʻāla. Replaced with hoʻāla, a common verified verb not in the list. Meaning: "to renew, to redo" → "to wake someone up; to revive".
- **v427 hoʻokāʻoi** → **hōʻoi**. hoʻokāʻoi does not mean to sharpen. Pukui-Elbert's only entry defines it as to progress, improve, and the English entry for sharpen gives hoʻokala and hōʻoi. Replaced with hōʻoi, the causative of ʻoi (428), which is verified and not in the list. Story kept, hook changed to match.

## Spelling fixes (22)

- **n008 makuakāne** → **makua kāne**. Pukui-Elbert writes father as two words, makua kāne, not makuakāne (no PE or MK headword for the one-word form). Updated headword, respelling, say_it and bonus; story already says 'Makua plus kāne'.
- **n018 hoaaloha** → **hoaloha**. Pukui-Elbert headword is hoaloha (also written hoa aloha); the one-word hoaaloha is the old Andrews/Parker spelling with no PE or MK entry. Changed headword, respelling, say_it and bonus. Story and hook ('Whoa, ah... ALOHA!') already fit ho-a-LO-ha.
- **n024 keikikāne** → **keiki kāne**. Pukui-Elbert writes boy as two words, keiki kāne; keikikāne as one word appears only in Andrews/Parker. Also added the common sense 'son'. Updated headword, respelling, say_it, bonus. Meaning: "boy" → "boy, son".
- **n035 mōʻīwahine** → **mōʻī wahine**. No dictionary has mōʻīwahine as one word; Pukui-Elbert has mōʻī wahine (two words). Updated headword, respelling, say_it and bonus; story already splits it as 'Mōʻī, wahine'.
- **n114 ipuhao** → **ipu hao**. Pukui-Elbert spells it as two words, ipu hao; the one-word ipuhao is only in Andrews, Parker and Judd. PE also widens it to any cooking pot or saucepan. Updated headword, respelling, say_it, bonus.
- **n135 palule** → **pālule**. Shirt is pālule with a long ā. Short-a palule is a different, rare word meaning commoner. Pronunciation, hook and story updated for the long ā.
- **n144 pulumi** → **pūlumi**. Broom is pūlumi with a long ū. Short-u pulumi means a flume or gutter.
- **n221 kāloti** → **kāloke**. kāloti is not in any Wehewehe dictionary (search returns "No entry found"). Pukui-Elbert spells carrot kāloke. Headword, pronunciation, hook, say_it, picture and story rewritten for kāloke.
- **n266 ʻānuenue** → **ānuenue**. Headword had an initial ʻokina. Pukui-Elbert spells rainbow ānuenue, with no ʻokina (as in the name of Ke Kula Kaiapuni ʻo Ānuenue).
- **n435 paʻiʻai** → **paʻi ʻai**. Pukui-Elbert spells the headword as two words, paʻi ʻai. The one word paʻiʻai is only in Judd/Pukui/Stokes (1943), so it is kept as a note in the bonus.
- **n460 kiʻipaʻi** → **kiʻi paʻi**. Māmaka Kaiao writes the word for photograph as two words, kiʻi paʻi. The one word form kiʻipaʻi is not in any dictionary.
- **n471 alahele** → **ala hele**. Pukui-Elbert writes this as two words, ala hele. The only one word alahele entry (Judd/Pukui/Stokes) means itinerary.
- **n477 ʻahamele** → **ʻaha mele**. One word ʻahamele is a needlefish in Pukui-Elbert. The word for concert is written as two words, ʻaha mele.
- **n495 hānauna** → **hanauna**. Pukui-Elbert spells generation hanauna, with no kahakō (hānau, to give birth, has one, but hanauna does not). Pronunciation, hook and story updated so they do not teach a long ā.
- **v121 heʻenalu** → **heʻe nalu**. Pukui-Elbert spells the surfing word as two words, heʻe nalu (the one-word form appears only in older dictionaries without the modern marks). Headword and respelling changed to match; the one-word form is mentioned in the bonus.
- **v122 lelekawa** → **lele kawa**. Pukui-Elbert spells this as two words, lele kawa, and defines it as a feet-first leap into water without splashing. The story had a giant splash, the opposite of the ideal. Headword, respelling, bonus and the splash scene corrected.
- **v232 hoʻomaikaʻi**. Story and picture spell the tuna as "ahi", which is the Hawaiian word for fire. The fish is ʻahi.
- **v249 kuʻu**. Story spells the tuna as "ahi" (fire). The fish is ʻahi.
- **v326 pākī** → **pakī**. Headword spelled pākī (long ā), which in Pukui-Elbert means to smash or crush. The splash/squirt word is pakī with a short a. Pronunciation, hook, say_it and story stretched the long ā, so they taught the wrong spelling.
- **v368 kōlohe** → **kolohe**. Headword spelled kōlohe with a kahakō. Every dictionary spells it kolohe with a short o. Hook and story stretched the first o ("Gooo"), teaching the wrong spelling.
- **v437 ʻalunu** → **ʻālunu**. Pukui-Elbert spells the word ʻālunu with a kahakō on the first a (Kent agrees). Spelling, pronunciation, hook and story updated.
- **v481 ololī** → **ʻololī**. Pukui-Elbert spells the word ʻololī with an initial ʻokina (Parker and Judd agree). Headword, story and bonus updated. Pronunciation unchanged.

## Meaning fixes (7)

- **n052 ʻōiwi**. Gloss 'native bone' is not a dictionary sense. PE gives native, native son (also physique, self); Māmaka Kaiao gives indigenous, native. The iwi link stays in the bonus, where PE supports it with 'Cf. iwi, bone'. Also fixed okole to ʻōkole in the story. Meaning: "native; native bone" → "native, indigenous".
- **n459 lako**. Lako alone means supplies, provisions, gear. Furniture is lako hale (furniture and fixtures for a house), so 'furniture' was taken out of the gloss and moved to the bonus. Meaning: "supplies, equipment, furniture" → "supplies, equipment, gear".
- **v162 kāhiko**. Gloss "to decorate" belongs to the causative hoʻokāhiko. Kāhiko itself is intransitive (to wear finery, dress up) or a noun (finery, ornaments), so a learner would misuse it as "to decorate something". Gloss narrowed and the one story line that had someone decorating the car now has the car itself dressed up. Meaning: "to dress up, to decorate" → "to dress up, to wear finery".
- **v317 hoʻoili**. Gloss "to unload" is not a dictionary sense. Pukui-Elbert gives hoʻoili as to load (freight on a ship), to transfer, to set on shore, and to bequeath. Māmaka Kaiao adds to transfer funds, to download, to hand off. The bonus claim that hoʻouka is load and hoʻoili is unload is unsupported. Story now acts out handing over (transfer) and passing down. Meaning: "to unload; to pass down" → "to transfer, to hand over; to pass down".
- **v393 pīhoihoi**. Gloss led with startled, which is closer to hikilele. Pukui-Elbert's core sense of pīhoihoi is disturbed, excited, worried, agitated, alarmed. Meaning: "to be startled, excited" → "to be excited, upset, alarmed".
- **v409 ake**. Gloss and story framed ake as the sad ache of missing something. Pukui-Elbert's sense is to desire, wish, be eager, yearn. The story keeps the slippah but now teaches wanting something so much it aches. Meaning: "to long for, to yearn" → "to desire, to yearn, to be eager".
- **v500 manawaleʻa**. The dictionaries do not give to volunteer for manawaleʻa (Pukui-Elbert's English entry for volunteer is ʻaʻa). Its sense is to give freely and willingly, generous, charity. The bonus also said manawa means time, but here it is Pukui-Elbert's manawa of heart or feelings. Gloss, bonus, say it and story updated so the trick teaches giving freely. Meaning: "to give freely, to volunteer" → "to give freely, to be generous".

## Pronunciation fixes (11)

- **n001 kanaka**. Three-syllable kanaka takes stress on the second-to-last syllable (ka-NA-ka), not the first. Fixed respelling, say_it and the caps in the sound hook; story words unchanged.
- **n316 ʻīlioholoikauaua**. Respelling 'kow-OW-ah' ran the vowels of 'ka uaua' together wrongly. Uaua is u.a.u.a (OO-ah-OO-ah), stressed on each u, so the ending should be kah-oo-ah-OO-ah.
- **n385 waena**. ae in waena is a diphthong stressed on its first vowel (WAE.na), so the stress falls on the first syllable, not on EH.
- **n436 ʻōʻio**. ʻōʻio has three vowels (ō, i, o) but the respelling oh-OH-ee-oh adds a fourth. Stress falls on the ʻi (ʻō.ʻio). Hook, say_it and story shouts fixed to match.
- **n451 loulu**. ou is a diphthong stressed on its first vowel, so loulu is LOU-lu, not lo-OO-loo. Respelled in the batch's style for ou (compare kou KO-oo).
- **v009 hāʻule**. Long ā was not shown as long (HAH). House style shows ā as aah, and the story itself stresses the long ā.
- **v036 kamaʻilio**. Stress was placed on the ʻi syllable (mah-EE). Pukui-Elbert divides the word kamaʻi.lio, so the stresses fall on MA and on LI.
- **v079 waiho**. Respelling used the Hawaiian letters WAI, which English readers tend to say as "way." The sound is "why," as the sound hook itself shows.
- **v224 paio**. Respelling pah-EE-oh splits ai into two syllables and stresses the i, which also clashes with the PIE! OH! hook. The ai is a diphthong here, so the word is two syllables with stress on PAI.
- **v358 ʻōʻili**. Stress was placed on the first syllable. Pukui-Elbert divides the word ʻō.ʻili, so the main stress falls on the ʻi of ʻili (same pattern as ʻā.pono, aah-PO-no). Bonus also described hoʻoili as to unload, which is not a dictionary sense.
- **v465 ʻōmaʻomaʻo**. Say it and story wrote the long ō as Ooh/Ooooh, which reads as an oo sound. ʻŌ is a long o (oh).

## Respect fixes (2)

- **n489 ʻōkole**. Bonus presents ʻōkole maluna as a plain local toast. Pukui-Elbert notes older Hawaiians condemn it as vulgar because of the sacredness of the body, and that ʻōkole is less polite than lemu. Learners should know that.
- **v474 ʻilihune**. Story placed the poverty joke in Kalihi, a real working class neighborhood, which can read as mocking its residents. Location removed, nothing else changed.

## Story fixes (5)

- **n255 mokupuni**. The closing line taught that mokupuni means a very small island. Mokupuni is simply the general word for island, of any size.
- **n448 ʻuku**. Story states as fact that the ʻukulele was named 'jumping flea' because fingers jump like fleas. That is one popular explanation among several, so it is now framed that way.
- **v004 lele**. Story stated as fact that ʻukulele means "jumping flea" because of the jumping, presenting one popular explanation of the name as settled. Softened so the literal parts are stated and the origin is framed as one explanation.
- **v016 ʻaki**. ʻAki is a quick nip that lets go, but the story had the crab biting down harder and hanging off your hand, which acts out a full bite (nahu). Bonus claimed nahu is a harder bite like a centipede; Pukui-Elbert gives nahu as the general word to bite, as a dog, with no centipede mention.
- **v429 hoʻomakeʻaka**. Story taught makeʻaka alone as a word meaning funny. The dictionaries only give it inside hoʻomākeʻaka / hoʻomakeʻaka (Pukui-Elbert lists -mākeʻaka as a bound form), so that line was removed.

- **v357 hoʻokupu**. Bonus presented an interpretation (a gift that helps something grow, like respect) as fact. It is a popular explanation built on kupu, to sprout, and needs to be framed that way.
- **v377 hoʻomanawanui**. Bonus stated as fact that manawa here means time. In manawanui the manawa is Pukui-Elbert's sense of heart or disposition (as in manawaleʻa); the time reading is only a popular memory aid.

- **v392 ukiuki**. Bonus implied ukiuki is only mild annoyance that turns into huhū. Pukui-Elbert includes anger and resentment in ukiuki itself.
- **v403 uhi**. Bonus called uhi the Hawaiian yam. Pukui-Elbert describes it as a yam from southeast Asia, carried through the Pacific, not a native Hawaiian plant.
- **v408 ʻiʻini**. Bonus said ake is a softer, sadder longing. Pukui-Elbert glosses ake simply as to desire, wish, be eager, yearn, with no sad or softer shade.
- **v415 mimiki**. Gloss and story said the tide pulls back. The dictionary sense is the sea or a wave receding, or an undertow; kai mimiki is a sea stirred up as by an earthquake, not the tide. Meaning: "to pull back (like the tide)" → "to recede, to pull back (of the sea)".
- **v476 hohonu**. Bonus said deep in thought. Pukui-Elbert gives profound, as in deep knowledge, not being lost in thought.
- **v490 hūpō**. Bonus called hūpō silly. Pukui-Elbert's sense is ignorant, unintelligent, stupid, literally swelling darkness.
- **v498 ani**. Bonus said peʻahi is fanning hard, which the dictionary does not say.

## Stress marks

27 respellings had the capital letters (main stress) on the wrong syllable, mostly words that start with a long vowel. They now follow Pukui-Elbert's syllable groups, for example kāhea is kaah-HEH-ah and kōkua is koh-KOO-ah.

## Spelling cleanups across stories

- Tutu is written Tūtū everywhere.
- ukulele is written ʻukulele, and the "jumping flea" origin is presented as one popular explanation.
- Tuna is ʻahi (ahi means fire). Kālua and ʻōkole got their marks.
- eʻe (to board) follows Pukui-Elbert's spelling.

## Still worth a native speaker's ear

- Stress on a few words where dictionaries differ or show no syllable dots: paia, naʻau, kaomi, ʻalae, poina, Pōʻaono.
